# **CHAPTER FIVE** {#chapter-five}

## **SUMMARY, CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS** {#summary,-conclusion-and-recommendations}

This chapter concludes the thesis by synthesising the research undertaken across the preceding four chapters. Section 5.1 summarises the research problem, methodology, and the key results obtained from the implementation and evaluation of the secure chat application. Section 5.2 draws conclusions regarding the extent to which the research aim, objectives, and research questions established in Chapter One have been achieved. Section 5.3 presents recommendations arising from the study, directed at developers, organisations, and policymakers concerned with secure communication. Section 5.4 acknowledges the limitations inherent in the research design and implementation. Section 5.5 outlines directions for future work that extend naturally from the findings and constraints of this study.

 

## **5.1 Summary** {#5.1-summary}

This research set out to design, implement, and evaluate a secure chat application with end-to-end encryption capable of addressing the persistent cryptographic weaknesses documented in widely deployed messaging platforms (Albrecht et al., 2022; Albrecht, Dowling, & Jones, 2023), the unresolved tension between key verification security and usability (Turner et al., 2023; Reuter et al., 2021), and the scalability limitations of pairwise group encryption schemes (Barnes et al., 2023). Chapter One established the background, problem statement, six research questions, aim, objectives, scope, significance, and limitations of the study, situating the research within the broader evolution of applied cryptography from the Diffie-Hellman key exchange (Diffie & Hellman, 1976) to the Signal Protocol (Marlinspike & Perrin, 2016) and the standardisation of Messaging Layer Security (Barnes et al., 2023).

Chapter Two reviewed recent literature published from 2021 to date across six thematic areas: end-to-end encryption concepts, cryptographic protocols, key management, attack vectors, secure messaging architectures, group messaging encryption, message authentication, and performance evaluation. The review identified four principal gaps: the scarcity of complete, documented reference implementations integrating formally verified E2EE components; the absence of deployed applications combining automated key verification with usable interfaces; the limited evaluation of MLS-based group messaging within complete application architectures; and the unresolved engineering challenge of integrating post-quantum constructions at the application level. These gaps directly motivated the design decisions taken in this research.

Chapter Three presented the Design Science Research methodology adopted for the study, detailing the conceptual framework, the three-tier system architecture, the justification for selecting the Signal Protocol (X3DH and the Double Ratchet Algorithm), AES-256-GCM, Ed25519, X25519, and an MLS-aligned TreeKEM scheme, the key management lifecycle design, the group messaging encryption design, the implementation environment (Spring Boot 3.2 on Java 21, React.js v18, Bouncy Castle, PostgreSQL/H2), and the security and performance evaluation frameworks used to assess the resulting artefact.

Chapter Four presented the results of the implementation and evaluation. The system was successfully implemented and compiled without errors, with the Spring Boot server starting in 5.753 seconds. The React.js frontend, illustrated in Figures 4.1 and 4.2, delivers a chat interface that communicates the active cryptographic protocols to the user at every stage of interaction, from account registration through to per-conversation safety number verification. Performance benchmarking across 1,000 iterations per test demonstrated that all cryptographic primitives operate well within interactive messaging latency requirements: AES-256-GCM encrypted a typical 256-byte message in a mean of 0.033 ms, X25519 key agreement completed in 0.184 ms, the full X3DH handshake completed in 0.775 ms, the Double Ratchet's per-message overhead was 0.030 ms, Ed25519 signing and verification completed in 0.172 ms and 0.179 ms respectively, and HKDF-SHA256 derivation completed in 0.006 ms. The TreeKEM group messaging scheme demonstrated O(log n) scaling for membership operations, with member addition rising from 0.140 ms at 2 members to only 0.582 ms at 100 members, while per-message group encryption remained constant at approximately 0.02–0.04 ms regardless of group size. All seven security properties evaluated — forward secrecy, message authentication, MitM detection via safety numbers, replay prevention, AES-GCM authentication tag integrity, X3DH shared secret agreement, and group forward secrecy — passed validation. Subsequent live functional testing of the deployed application, conducted by registering accounts and exchanging messages between separate browser sessions, initially established that neither one-to-one nor group messaging were usable through the application: the recipient's chat view never displayed a message sent by the other party, since no message-decryption endpoint existed in the backend and the frontend neither retrieved chat history nor subscribed to the real-time message channel, while group messaging had no decryption endpoint or user interface of any kind. Both gaps were subsequently fixed. One-to-one delivery was corrected by mirroring the recipient's side of the Double Ratchet server-side and having the frontend poll and display decrypted chat history; group messaging was corrected by persisting group messages, adding a group-message decryption endpoint, and building a group-chat interface — creation, membership management, sending, and receiving. Both fixes were re-verified live: two-user bidirectional multi-message decryption for one-to-one chat, and three-user group messaging with a member removal that correctly advanced the group's epoch and removed the group from the departed member's own group list. A subsequent, dedicated client-side crypto migration then moved key generation, X3DH session establishment, the Double Ratchet, and Ed25519 signing for one-to-one messaging from the Spring Boot backend into the React client, and was likewise re-verified live: two fresh accounts confirmed client-side session establishment and bidirectional encrypted messaging with the ratchet advancing correctly across messages, and inspection of the H2 console confirmed that the server retains only public keys and opaque ciphertext for 1:1 conversations, with no private keys or plaintext held server-side. Group messaging was out of scope for this migration and remains server-side. Against the evaluation framework defined in Chapter Three, four of the six research objectives (RQ1, for one-to-one messaging; RQ2; RQ4; and RQ6) were confirmed as met, and RQ3 and RQ5 remain assessed as partially met — key rotation and revocation remain unimplemented, the TreeKEM group scheme remains a simplified, non-persistent approximation of full MLS, and group messaging's server-side key custody remains open, unaffected by the migration, as a separate future sub-project. These findings are detailed in Chapter Four, Section 4.9, and in Sections 5.4 and 5.5 below.

 

## **5.2 Conclusion** {#5.2-conclusion}

This research has demonstrated that a secure chat application providing robust, formally grounded end-to-end encryption can be designed and implemented using established, formally verified cryptographic protocols, while achieving sub-millisecond performance overhead and remaining accessible to non-expert users. Each research question posed in Chapter One is answered by the results presented in Chapter Four.

In relation to RQ1 and RQ2, the implemented system confirms that the Signal Protocol's Double Ratchet Algorithm combined with X25519-based ECDH key exchange correctly establishes session keys without any reliance on prior secret sharing, and that the resulting cryptographic primitives satisfy their formal security properties: the X3DH handshake, requiring four Diffie-Hellman operations and HKDF derivation, completes in a mean of 0.775 ms as a one-time session establishment cost, while the resulting Double Ratchet session encrypts each subsequent message with negligible overhead (0.030 ms mean). These results confirm the underlying algorithms in isolation. Live testing of the deployed application — registering two accounts, establishing a session between them, and sending a message from separate browser sessions — initially found that the recipient's chat view never displayed the message the other party had sent, and inspection of the codebase confirmed that no decryption endpoint existed for the recipient to invoke; the sending account's own display of the message was a local echo of the typed plaintext rather than a decrypted server response. This was remediated by having `MessageService` compute the recipient's side of the same X3DH handshake and Double Ratchet initialization the server already performs for the sender, so that a mirrored ratchet state is available to decrypt the recipient's messages on request, and by having the frontend poll and render the resulting decrypted chat history in place of the local echo. Re-testing with two fresh accounts confirmed that messages are now correctly delivered and decrypted in both directions across multiple messages. Verification of the codebase at that stage additionally established that key generation and ratchet/session operations for both parties executed within the Spring Boot backend rather than the browser client, meaning private key material for the entire conversation — not only the sender's — never left the server; on that evidence RQ1 was assessed as only partially met.

A subsequent, dedicated client-side crypto migration resolved this for one-to-one messaging: key generation, X3DH session establishment, the Double Ratchet, and Ed25519 signing were moved into the React client, and the server was changed so that it persists only public keys and opaque ciphertext for 1:1 conversations. Live verification with two fresh accounts across separate browser sessions confirmed the migration — registration and session establishment ran client-side, messages sent from each account were correctly encrypted and decrypted client-side by the other, a second message confirmed that the Double Ratchet chain key advances correctly across multiple messages, and inspection of the H2 console confirmed that no private keys or plaintext reached the server for either account. On this evidence, RQ1 is assessed as met for one-to-one messaging: both the functional requirement that communicating parties can access message content, and the stronger guarantee that only communicating parties — and not the server — can do so, are now satisfied for this messaging mode. This assessment holds notwithstanding the continued use of client-side polling rather than real-time push for delivery, which is a separate, explicitly scoped-out aspect of the system (Section 5.5, item 10) rather than a gap in the E2EE guarantee itself.

This resolution does not extend to group messaging, whose server-side key custody remains exactly as originally implemented — TreeKEM group creation, membership management, and epoch-key derivation continue to execute server-side — and is tracked as a separate, not-yet-started future sub-project (Section 5.5). RQ2, which concerns the correctness of the ECDH mechanism itself, is assessed as met at the protocol-implementation level, and now also at the deployed-system level for one-to-one messaging. These findings are documented in Chapter Four, Section 4.9, and Section 5.5. In relation to RQ3, the key management module was confirmed to automate key generation, distribution, and safety-number-based verification, with the safety number mechanism providing users with a transparent, out-of-band method of detecting man-in-the-middle attacks that directly addresses the usability concerns raised by Turner et al. (2023) and Reuter et al. (2021) by embedding verification within the conversation view rather than a separate settings interface. Key rotation and revocation, though specified in the original objectives, were not implemented in the current codebase; RQ3 is therefore assessed as partially met. In relation to RQ4, the combination of Ed25519 digital signatures and AES-256-GCM's authentication tag was shown to reliably detect tampered messages while introducing under 0.2 ms of latency per operation, confirming robust message authentication and integrity enforcement and fully satisfying RQ4. In relation to RQ5, a simplified, epoch-based TreeKEM-aligned group messaging scheme was shown to preserve forward secrecy across dynamic group membership while scaling logarithmically rather than linearly with group size, validating the performance characteristics of the epoch-advancement approach for groups of at least 100 members as a standalone component. Live testing initially found that this scheme was not reachable through any user interface — the React frontend contained no group-chat functionality — and that no endpoint existed for a group member to decrypt a received group message. This was remediated by persisting group messages, adding a decryption endpoint that uses the group's current epoch key, and building a group-chat interface for creating groups, sending and receiving messages, and adding or removing members. Re-testing with three fresh accounts confirmed a message sent to the group was correctly decrypted by the other two members, and that removing a member advanced the group's epoch and immediately removed the group from the departed member's own group list — confirming group forward secrecy holds in the live application. The underlying limitation that motivated the original design choice remains, however: epoch keys are still derived from centrally generated randomness rather than per-member path-secret encapsulation, and group membership/epoch state is held only in memory, unlike the group messages themselves, which are now persisted — meaning a server restart would leave stored group ciphertext with no key material anywhere to decrypt it. RQ5 is therefore assessed as partially met: group messaging is now usable end-to-end, but the design falls short of full MLS conformance and state durability. In relation to RQ6, the performance and security evaluation confirms that the developed application's cryptographic primitives achieve encryption overhead, latency, forward secrecy, and MitM resistance properties that compare favourably with the theoretical guarantees of the underlying protocols and with published characteristics of existing solutions such as Signal and MLS-based systems (Ueda et al., 2024), fully satisfying RQ6.

The study concludes that the persistent vulnerabilities documented in custom-protocol messaging systems (Albrecht et al., 2022; Albrecht, Dowling, & Jones, 2023) are not an inevitable consequence of building secure chat applications, but rather a consequence of avoiding formally verified, community-audited cryptographic constructions. By adopting the Signal Protocol, AES-256-GCM, Ed25519, X25519, and an MLS-aligned TreeKEM design — all subject to rigorous independent formal analysis (Bienstock et al., 2022; Brendel et al., 2021; Wallez et al., 2023) — this research produced cryptographic primitives that are correctly implemented, formally grounded, and achieve practical, real-time performance. The research therefore substantially fulfils its aim of designing, implementing, and evaluating a secure chat application with end-to-end encryption for both one-to-one and group messaging: live testing initially found that the deployed application did not function as a working two-way chat system for either case, and both were corrected during the course of verification, so that messages sent by one party are now correctly delivered to, decrypted by, and displayed for the other, confirmed across bidirectional one-to-one and three-member group re-tests, including a member-removal test that correctly revoked the removed member's access to the group. One respect in which the aim remains unfulfilled persists for group messaging: group creation, membership management, and epoch-key operations continue to execute on the server rather than the client, so the server retains the private key material and computational capability to decrypt group conversations. This is no longer true for one-to-one messaging: a subsequent client-side crypto migration, live-verified across two accounts, confirmed that key generation and ratchet operations for 1:1 conversations execute in the browser and that the server holds only public keys and ciphertext. Migrating group messaging's cryptographic operations to the client, replacing polling with real-time WebSocket delivery, implementing key rotation and key revocation, and bringing the TreeKEM group scheme to full per-member path-secret derivation with persistent state, as identified in Sections 5.4 and 5.5, remain necessary before the system constitutes a complete, genuinely end-to-end secure chat application across both messaging modes. The study nonetheless contributes a documented, quantitatively evaluated, and functionally working one-to-one and group messaging reference implementation to a research area in which complete, open reference implementations remain scarce.

 

## **5.3 Recommendations** {#5.3-recommendations}

Based on the findings of this research, the following recommendations are made:

**1. Adopt formally verified cryptographic protocols over custom implementations.** Developers of secure messaging systems should adopt established, formally verified protocols such as the Signal Protocol, AES-256-GCM, Ed25519, and MLS/TreeKEM rather than designing custom cryptographic schemes. The measured sub-millisecond performance of these constructions in this study demonstrates that formally verified protocols impose no meaningful performance penalty relative to the security assurances they provide, removing any practical justification for custom protocol design.

**2. Integrate automated key verification alongside manual safety number checks.** Given the well-documented usability challenges of manual key fingerprint verification (Turner et al., 2023), organisations deploying secure messaging systems should implement automated key change detection, building on the safety-number mechanism demonstrated in this system's key management module, as a complementary safeguard rather than relying on user-initiated verification alone.

**3. Present cryptographic status transparently within the user interface.** The interface developed in this study communicates active encryption protocols directly within the authentication and chat views. Secure messaging applications should similarly surface encryption status and key verification affordances contextually, within the conversation flow, rather than isolating them within secondary settings screens that users seldom visit (Reuter et al., 2021).

**4. Design group messaging with logarithmic-complexity key management from the outset.** Applications intending to support group conversations should adopt TreeKEM/MLS-aligned designs rather than pairwise or Sender Keys-based schemes, given the demonstrated O(log n) scalability advantage and the documented post-compromise security weaknesses of Sender Keys-based approaches (Alwen, Jost, & Mularczyk, 2022).

**5. Institutions and regulators should encourage the use of independently audited cryptographic libraries.** Given the demonstrated risks of custom protocol implementations (Albrecht et al., 2022), regulatory and institutional guidance for organisations deploying messaging systems, particularly within the Nigerian public and financial sectors, should mandate the use of formally reviewed cryptographic libraries such as Bouncy Castle, libsodium, or equivalent audited implementations.

 

## **5.4 Limitations** {#5.4-limitations}

While this research achieved its stated aim and objectives, several limitations, consistent with those anticipated in Section 1.7, constrain the generalisability of the findings.

**1. Controlled laboratory evaluation.** All performance and security evaluations reported in Chapter Four were conducted in a controlled development environment on a single machine configuration (Windows 11, multi-core processor, Java 25 LTS). The results do not account for the variability of real-world networks, heterogeneous client devices, or the concurrency demands of a production-scale user base, and may therefore differ under real-world deployment conditions.

**2. Text-based messaging only.** The implemented system encrypts and secures only text messages. Voice messages, images, video, and file transfer were excluded from the implementation scope, and the performance and security properties reported in this study cannot be assumed to extend automatically to multimedia content, which introduces distinct cryptographic and performance considerations.

**3. Absence of formal usability testing.** Although the interface was designed with the usability findings of Turner et al. (2023) and Reuter et al. (2021) in mind, no structured human-subject usability study was conducted to empirically measure how non-expert users interact with the key verification and management features. The usability claims made in this study are therefore design-informed rather than empirically validated through user testing.

**4. Evaluation limited to documented attack vectors.** The security evaluation in Section 4.7 tested the system against seven documented and well-understood attack categories drawn from the reviewed literature. The system has not been evaluated against novel or currently unknown attack techniques, and no mechanism exists within the implementation to adapt to vulnerabilities discovered after completion of this research.

**5. Partial post-quantum readiness.** While the architectural design acknowledges the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat and the emergence of PQXDH and CRYSTALS-Kyber (Bhargavan et al., 2024; NIST, 2024), the implemented system relies exclusively on classical elliptic-curve cryptography (X25519, Ed25519). Full post-quantum key agreement was not implemented, leaving the system exposed to future quantum adversaries capable of retrospectively decrypting recorded traffic.

**6. Server-side execution of cryptographic operations — resolved for one-to-one messaging.** Verification of the implementation against the codebase initially established that key generation, X3DH session establishment, Double Ratchet encryption/decryption, and Ed25519 message signing were executed within the Spring Boot backend rather than within the React.js client, and that user private keys were generated and persisted server-side, with the React frontend containing no cryptographic code of its own — meaning the deployed reference system did not provide the architectural guarantee, central to the concept of end-to-end encryption, that only communicating parties, and not the server operator, can access message content. This was subsequently resolved for one-to-one messaging by a dedicated client-side crypto migration: key generation, X3DH session establishment, the Double Ratchet, and Ed25519 signing were moved into the React client, and the server was changed to persist only public keys and opaque ciphertext for 1:1 conversations. Live verification with two fresh accounts confirmed the migration, including — via the H2 console — that no private keys or plaintext reach the server for 1:1 messaging. This resolution is scoped to one-to-one messaging only: group messaging's TreeKEM operations (group creation, membership management, and epoch-key derivation) remain unaffected and continue to execute server-side, so the custody limitation described above remains true for group conversations and is tracked as a separate, not-yet-started future sub-project (Section 5.5).

**7. Single-device key storage with no backup or recovery mechanism.** The client-side crypto migration undertaken for one-to-one messaging (item 6 above; Section 5.5, item 8) stores each user's identity, signed-prekey, and one-time-prekey private keys, together with per-conversation Double Ratchet state, exclusively within the browser on the device where the account was registered, with no mechanism to back up, export, or synchronise this key material to another device or browser profile. This is an inherent consequence of removing private key custody from the server rather than a defect introduced by the migration, but it is a genuine trade-off: clearing browser storage, switching browsers or devices, or otherwise losing the local key store leaves the user with no way to recover the identity or decrypt prior one-to-one conversations on that device, and requires generating a new identity to continue using the application. A production deployment would need to address this, for example through an encrypted, user-controlled key-backup mechanism.

**8. Incomplete key lifecycle and simplified, non-persistent group key management.** The key management module implements key generation, distribution, and safety-number verification, but does not implement key rotation or revocation, despite these being specified among the original research objectives. Similarly, the TreeKEM-based group messaging scheme derives new epoch secrets from centrally generated server-side randomness rather than through per-member path-secret encapsulation, meaning group members cannot independently re-derive updated group keys as a fully conformant MLS/RFC 9420 implementation would require. Group membership and epoch state are also held only in memory (`GroupService`) and are not persisted across server restarts — a limitation that became more consequential once group messages themselves were made persistent (Section 5.4, item 9): a server restart would now leave stored group ciphertext with no key material anywhere able to decrypt it, rather than simply losing an in-memory conversation that was never saved.

**9. Message and group delivery — both identified as non-functional, both subsequently fixed.** Live testing of the deployed application initially found that neither one-to-one nor group messages were actually delivered between users. For one-to-one messages: the recipient's chat view never displayed a message sent by the other party, since the backend implemented `DoubleRatchet.decrypt()` but never called it from any endpoint, the recipient's prekey private keys were discarded rather than persisted (so their side of the ratchet could not even be reconstructed), and the React frontend neither retrieved stored chat history nor subscribed to the real-time WebSocket channel. This was fixed by having `MessageService` mirror the recipient's X3DH and Double Ratchet state server-side at session establishment, adding a decrypt-and-cache step (with Ed25519 signature verification) to the chat history endpoint, and having the frontend poll and render that decrypted history in place of the previous local echo. For group messages: `GroupService.sendGroupMessage()` encrypted messages but never persisted them, `decryptGroupMessage()` was never exposed by any controller, and the frontend had no group-chat interface at all. This was fixed by persisting group messages, adding a decrypt-and-cache endpoint using the group's current epoch key, and building a group-chat interface — group creation, an automatically populated list of the groups a user belongs to, sending and receiving messages, and adding or removing members through a members panel. Re-testing confirmed both fixes: correct bidirectional, multi-message delivery for one-to-one chat across two fresh accounts, and correct three-member group delivery with a member-removal test that advanced the group's epoch and immediately removed the group from the departed member's own group list. One thing remains unaddressed by both fixes: delivery is now driven by two-second polling rather than the real-time WebSocket push the architecture was designed around.

 

## **5.5 Future Work** {#5.5-future-work}

The findings and limitations of this research suggest several directions for future work:

**1. Post-quantum protocol migration.** Future work should integrate PQXDH, combining classical X25519 with CRYSTALS-Kyber (ML-KEM), into the existing key exchange layer, following the formally verified specification of Bhargavan et al. (2024) and the standardised NIST FIPS 203 construction (NIST, 2024). The performance impact of hybrid post-quantum key encapsulation, as characterised by Kim et al. (2025), should be benchmarked against the classical baseline established in this study.

**2. Empirical usability evaluation.** A structured, human-subject usability study, following the methodology of Turner et al. (2023), should be conducted to empirically assess how effectively non-expert users understand and engage with the safety number verification and automated key change detection features implemented in this system.

**3. Real-world and multi-device deployment testing.** Future work should evaluate the system under production network conditions, including variable latency, packet loss, and concurrent multi-device sessions, extending the controlled benchmark results of Chapter Four to realistic deployment scenarios, and addressing the multi-device key synchronisation challenge examined by Davies et al. (2023).

**4. Extension to multimedia messaging.** The cryptographic and architectural framework developed in this study should be extended to support encrypted voice, image, video, and file transfer, requiring additional consideration of streaming encryption, chunked AEAD constructions, and the performance implications of larger payloads.

**5. Metadata protection mechanisms.** Building on the findings of Domenech et al. (2022) regarding metadata leakage in instant messaging, future work should investigate traffic padding, timing normalisation, and sealed-sender techniques to minimise the residual metadata exposure that persists even when message content is fully end-to-end encrypted.

**6. Large-scale group messaging evaluation.** While this study benchmarked TreeKEM group operations up to 100 members, future work should extend this evaluation to groups of several hundred or thousand members, following the methodology of Ueda et al. (2024), to characterise commit latency and identify the scalability ceiling of the current implementation under high membership churn.

**7. Continuous security monitoring for emerging attack vectors.** Future iterations of the system should incorporate a continuous security review process, informed by ongoing formal analyses of deployed messaging protocols, to ensure that newly discovered vulnerabilities are identified and remediated beyond the fixed set of attack vectors evaluated in this research.

**8. Migration of cryptographic operations to the client — completed for one-to-one messaging.** This migration has since been carried out for one-to-one messaging: key generation, X3DH session establishment, Double Ratchet encryption/decryption, and Ed25519 signing now execute within the React.js client rather than the Spring Boot backend, so private key material for 1:1 conversations never leaves the user's device. This was confirmed live with two fresh accounts, including verification via the H2 console that the server retains only public keys and opaque ciphertext for 1:1 conversations (Section 5.4, item 6), resolving the server-side key custody limitation identified there for one-to-one messaging specifically. The equivalent migration for group messaging — moving TreeKEM group creation, membership management, and epoch-key derivation into the client — was out of scope for this migration, remains unaffected, and is tracked as a separate, not-yet-started future sub-project; it would need to address its own design questions, such as per-member path-secret encapsulation (item 9 below), alongside the custody change.

**9. Full key lifecycle and conformant TreeKEM implementation.** Future work should implement key rotation (periodic replacement of identity and signed prekeys) and key revocation (invalidation of a compromised key with propagation to all contacts), both specified in the original objectives but absent from the current codebase. The TreeKEM group messaging implementation should also be extended to perform per-member path-secret encapsulation and persistent group state storage, bringing it into full conformance with RFC 9420 rather than the simplified, centrally-derived epoch key scheme evaluated in this study.

**10. Real-time message push.** One-to-one and group message delivery and decryption were both implemented and verified during this study (Section 5.4, item 9), but delivery for both currently relies on the frontend polling `/api/chat/history` and `/api/groups/{groupId}/messages` every two seconds rather than genuine push delivery — unaffected by the client-side crypto migration completed for one-to-one messaging (item 8 above). Future work should replace this with push delivery over the already-configured STOMP/SockJS WebSocket channel, so the server can push ciphertext for on-device decryption (already the case for 1:1 messaging following item 8) rather than pushing plaintext itself, and should extend the same pattern to group messaging once its cryptographic operations are likewise migrated to the client.
